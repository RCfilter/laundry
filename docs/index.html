Start here

If you’ve been tossing Tide Pods in and calling it a day — and that works for you — you don’t have to change anything. Many people never notice a problem until they get new machines, harder water, or gym clothes that won’t stop smelling.

This guide explains why enthusiasts use different products for different loads, and gives you enough chemistry to make informed choices without memorizing a chemistry degree.

The one-sentence version Use liquid when oils and grease are the main problem; use powder when dirt, mud, dinginess, and odors are the main problem. Most beginners do fine with a good liquid alone; adding powder (or boosters) helps for whites, towels, and heavily soiled work clothes.
Problem Reach for Examples
Oils, grease, food stains, body oils Liquid detergent (can pre-treat stains directly) Everyday clothes, gym wear, shirts with spills
Dirt, mud, dust, dinginess, whitening, stubborn odors Powder detergent (often has oxygen bleach / activators) Work clothes, gardening clothes, white towels & sheets

Liquid vs powder

When liquid shines

Liquids are already dissolved, so they’re especially good at:

  • Body oils and sweat
  • Food grease, makeup, sunscreen
  • Motor oil and similar oily stains

You can also apply liquid directly to a stain before washing.

When powder shines

Powders often contain ingredients that excel at:

  • Clay, mud, dust, and soil
  • Soot, ash, and general dinginess
  • Whitening whites and removing stubborn odors

Many powders include oxygen bleach or bleach activators for whitening and deodorizing.

Temperature & hardness (quick rules)

  • Cold water: liquids generally dissolve and work well. Modern powders are much better than old formulas, but in very cold water, make sure powder fully dissolves.
  • Warm/hot: traditional powders loved warm water; oxygen bleach works best warm-to-hot with enough time.
  • Hard water: powders often perform well (many include water-softening ingredients). Some liquids struggle depending on formula.
  • Soft water: both usually work fine.

What’s in your detergent

Understanding a few ingredient types helps you read labels and fix problems (especially gym odors).

Surfactants

Surface-active agents — the primary cleaners. They lift and carry soil; they don’t chemically destroy stains. Great for everyday dirt, body oils, and grease. They prevent soil from reattaching to fabric during the wash.

Enzymes

EnzymeTargets
ProteaseSweat proteins, blood, food proteins
LipaseBody oils, sebum, greasy residues
AmylaseStarch-based food stains
CellulaseCan reduce dinginess and improve fabric appearance
DNaseHelps remove oily residues from humans/animals (found in some sport detergents)

Oxygen bleach

Usually sodium percarbonate, which releases hydrogen peroxide in water. It chemically changes stains (breaks color bonds) rather than just lifting them. Best on organic stains (wine, coffee, grass, blood), yellowed whites, and odor-causing residues. Less effective on grease alone. Warm-to-hot water and soak time improve results. Color-safe on most fabrics.

Oxi boosters (e.g. Biz)

Boost cleaning, soften water, and improve stain/soil removal. Often contain oxygen bleach and sometimes optical brighteners (OBAs) — great for whites and lights, but OBAs can fade colored clothing over long periods. For darks darker than “light navy,” consider a dark-safe oxi without OBAs (e.g. Febu) or skip oxi on those loads.

Application tip: add oxi to the drum before clothes. In warm+ water it can go in dry; in cooler temps, dissolve in a quart of hot tap water first.

Citric acid (rinse aid)

Used in the rinse, not as detergent. Balances pH, helps remove excess detergent and mineral film, and can improve fabric feel (fluffier towels, smoother sheets). Odor benefits often come from stripping sticky detergent/mineral buildup rather than masking smell.

Dose starting point: 1–2 teaspoons in the softener drawer. Increase for very hard water (roughly +50% base dose per 100 ppm hardness past ~150 ppm). Most HE machines tolerate dry powder in the dispenser; if you see residue, top off with tap water after adding acid, or dissolve in room-temperature water first (add acid to water, not water to acid).

Water, temperature & HE machines

High-efficiency (HE) washers

Both liquid and powder work in HE machines if labeled HE-compatible. The bigger issue is dosage:

  • Too much liquid → residue on clothes
  • Too much powder → undissolved detergent

Most people use more detergent than necessary. When tuning a new routine, watch suds: a trace of bubbles on the door glass a few minutes into agitation is enough; more than that → reduce dose.

Hard water dosing (example from community)

At ~50 ppm hardness, ~¼ cup oxi per “default” load is a starting point; at ~105 ppm, ~⅓ cup may be appropriate. Add roughly a tablespoon more oxi per 50 ppm hardness beyond ~75 ppm, then adjust if suds are excessive.

A practical detergent setup

If you want to keep it simple

A good liquid detergent is the most versatile single choice for everyday laundry, dark clothes, and oily stains (including spot treatment).

If you want enthusiast-level results

Many people keep both:

ProductUse for
LiquidDaily laundry, darks, oily stains, pre-treating
PowderWhites, towels, bedding, muddy/heavily soiled loads, odor-heavy items

If you regularly wash work clothes, sports uniforms, gardening clothes, or white linens, keeping powder on hand is often worth it even if liquid is your default.

Gym clothes & enzyme boosters

Gym wear problems are usually:

  1. Body oils/sebum trapped in synthetic fibers
  2. Sweat residues
  3. Odor-causing bacteria feeding on what’s left behind

Do enzyme boosters (e.g. Dirty Labs Bio Enzyme Booster) help?

Yes, often — especially for body oils and gym clothes — but how much depends on what you’re removing and what detergent you already use.

  • Enzymes break down soils; they don’t magically destroy odors in one wash.
  • Lipase is the key enzyme for body oils — critical for polyester workout shirts, leggings, and sports bras where oil builds up over many washes.
  • If you already use a premium enzyme-rich powder, a booster may only help modestly.
  • Boosters help more when: your detergent has limited enzymes, you wash cold, odors persist, or older synthetics seem “permanently funky.”
Expectation vs reality Effects are usually cumulative: odors fade faster over time, buildup slows, stubborn items become easier to restore. One scoop rarely makes a rank shirt “brand new.”

Bigger wins than adding a booster

  • Don’t overdose detergent (residue traps odor)
  • Wash soon after workouts
  • Use a detergent with strong enzyme chemistry
  • Avoid fabric softener on athletic wear (coating traps oils and bacteria)
  • Occasionally deep-soak heavily affected items

Moving from a basic detergent to a high-quality enzyme detergent often helps more than stacking a booster on top of an already strong formula.

Why polyester is the worst offender

Polyester attracts and holds oily compounds more than cotton. The same wash that leaves cotton fine can leave microscopic oil in polyester — odor shows up when body heat warms the fabric and volatile compounds evaporate (“smelled clean out of the dryer, vomit smell 15 minutes into my workout”).

The “vomit smell” problem (butyric acid)

Clothes that smell sour, cheesy, or like vomit after washing usually don’t mean the detergent did nothing. Something organic survived the wash and was transformed or revealed — often by bacteria — into volatile fatty acids like butyric acid, detectable at extremely low concentrations.

Common causes

  1. Body oils not fully removed — sebum, sweat, skin cells, cosmetics trapped in fibers (especially polyester). Bacteria metabolize leftovers → butyric acid. Shirt smells fine wet, sour when dry or warmed.
  2. Biofilm in the washer — bacteria, fungi, soap scum, and oils in front-load gaskets and low-water HE systems can transfer back onto clothes. Machine may smell only mildly musty while clothes come out worse.
  3. Enzymes left intermediates behind — lipases split fats into fatty acids; proteases split proteins. Normally these rinse away. Overloaded washer, low dose, or poor rinse → smellier breakdown products remain.
  4. Anaerobic bacteria in damp laundry — clothes sitting in a hamper or washer overnight produce butyric acid, isovaleric acid, etc.
  5. Fabric softener buildup — hydrophobic coating traps oils and bacteria under a “clean” surface.

What actually helps

  • Correct detergent dose with enzymes (especially lipase)
  • Warm water when fabric care allows
  • No fabric softener on athletic wear
  • Extra rinse when needed
  • Periodic washer cleaning (tub clean, citric acid or tablets monthly)
  • Occasional oxygen bleach on appropriate loads
  • Don’t leave wet laundry sitting for hours

Example load-by-load routine

Products referenced: Biz (oxi), Whole Foods 365 Sport (liquid, DNase + lipase), 365 Unscented Powder (stronger surfactants, more enzymes, oxygen bleach), citric acid in softener drawer. Most loads use double extra rinse.

Why both Sport and Powder? 365 Sport brings DNase and lipase for athletic/soiled garments; 365 Powder brings stronger surfactants, additional enzymes, and oxygen bleach. Using both on every load (except delicates) is intentional overlap, not an accident — whether it’s overkill depends on your goals and budget. If cost doesn’t matter and you want maximum cleaning, the combo is reasonable; if simplifying, try powder-only on muddy/white loads and liquid-only on everyday darks.

Washer & dryer care

Washer

  • Extra rinse — helps remove detergent, bleach, and mineral residue (especially with boosters and hard water).
  • Monthly tub clean — cleaning tablets in drum, or powdered cleaner / citric acid in main detergent compartment (remove liquid cup if required by manual).
  • Weekly — wipe and disinfect door seal/gasket.
  • After each use — leave door and detergent drawer open to dry.
  • Monthly — remove and clean detergent drawer.
  • Periodically — clean drain pump filter.
  • After tub clean with descaler, run rinse+spin before the next wash if manual recommends it.

Dryer

  • Clean lint trap every load.
  • Clean vent ducts annually (fire safety + efficiency).
  • Medium heat is a common default; dryer sheets (e.g. lint-guard types) are optional — skip or minimize on athletic synthetics if odor is an issue.

FAQ

Is using both liquid Sport and powder on every load overkill?

Not necessarily. They cover different strengths (DNase/lipase sport liquid vs surfactants/enzymes/oxygen bleach in powder). If you’re not cost-sensitive and want maximum cleaning on most loads, using both is a valid plan. Skip powder on delicates; skip oxi with OBAs on darks. If you want simpler: liquid-only for daily/darks, add powder (+ oxi) for whites, towels, bedding, and heavy soil.

Should citric acid go dry in the softener drawer or dissolved in water?

Most HE machines handle dry citric acid in the softener compartment. If you see undissolved residue, top the compartment with tap water after adding the powder, or pre-dissolve in room-temperature water (citric acid into water, slowly). Warm wash water usually helps even if you add it dry.

Will an enzyme booster fix gym smell in one wash?

Usually no. Expect gradual improvement over multiple washes, plus fixes to dose, timing, softener use, and washer hygiene.

Why does my shirt smell fine in the dryer but awful when I exercise?

Classic sign of residual body oils in synthetic fibers. Body heat releases volatile fatty acids (including butyric acid) that were present but not obvious when cold.
